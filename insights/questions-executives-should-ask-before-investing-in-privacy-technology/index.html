Eight questions executives should ask before investing in privacy technology

By Dev SareenNovember 25, 20263 min read

Privacy technology decisions often start in a vendor demo and end in a budget approval. The questions that decide whether the investment pays off are rarely about features. They are about ownership, process and what happens after launch. These eight are worth asking before you commit.

1. What specific risk or decision does this improve?

Why it matters: Technology bought to "improve privacy" in general tends to be configured in general, and measured never.

A good answer sounds like: "It cuts the time to fulfill data subject requests and gives us evidence for regulators," or "It lets us see third-party privacy risk by tier."

2. Who will own it a year from now?

Why it matters: Projects have owners until they finish. Platforms need owners after.

A good answer sounds like: A named role, with time set aside for the job and authority to decide on changes.

3. Which processes will change, and are their owners involved?

Why it matters: A privacy platform changes how marketing, customer service, procurement and IT work. If those teams meet it for the first time at launch, adoption suffers.

A good answer sounds like: The affected process owners have helped design the workflows and agreed their part.

4. What do we already own?

Why it matters: Many organizations already have a privacy platform or other tools that cover part of the need. Buying something new before making the most of what exists adds cost and complexity.

A good answer sounds like: A clear view of current capabilities, what is unused, and the specific gaps a new purchase would close.

5. How will we know it is working?

Why it matters: "Go-live" is an event, not an outcome. Without measures, you cannot defend the budget or see problems early.

A good answer sounds like: Two or three measures agreed before launch, such as request turnaround, assessment cycle time or vendor coverage, reviewed on a regular rhythm.

6. How will it cope with laws that keep changing?

Why it matters: Requirements evolve. A setup tied tightly to today's rules becomes expensive to change.

A good answer sounds like: A common control set mapped to requirements, a regulatory horizon someone watches, and a plan for configuration updates.

7. What does it connect to, and who maintains the connections?

Why it matters: Value often depends on integrations with HR, CRM, ticketing, identity and data systems. Integrations break when upstream systems change.

A good answer sounds like: A list of connected systems, a named technical owner for each, and a way to find out when something fails.

8. What does it cost to run, beyond the license?

Why it matters: Licenses are the visible cost. Configuration, integration, ongoing tuning and the people time to operate the platform are often larger.

A good answer sounds like: A multi-year view that includes people, change management and support, not just the subscription.

Red flags

  • The decision rests mainly on a feature checklist.
  • No one can say who will own the platform after launch.
  • Success is defined as "launched".
  • The implementation plan has no post-launch phase.
  • Process owners outside the privacy team have not been consulted.

How to use these questions

Bring them to vendor meetings, steering committees and budget reviews. Strong answers do not need to be long, but they should be specific. If the answers are vague, treat that as useful information: it usually means the foundations need attention before the technology does.

Key takeaways

  • Tie the investment to a specific risk or decision, not to privacy in general.
  • Name the owner for after launch, and involve the teams whose work will change.
  • Check what you already own before buying more.
  • Agree outcome measures up front, and budget for running the platform, not just licensing it.
